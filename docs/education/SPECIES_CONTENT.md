# Species Content — Compiled Research

Research dossiers feeding the species pages defined in [SPECIES_TEMPLATE.md](SPECIES_TEMPLATE.md):
the current 12-species catalogue, recommended additions, the safety canon the section must teach,
and points where authorities genuinely disagree. Facts were cross-checked against reputable
sources (NCBI/StatPearls/CDC MMWR, FDA, university extension services, First Nature, MykoWeb,
UBC Beaty Museum, iNaturalist, NAMA); source URLs are inline.

**Sourcing standard for all future content:** use primary/expert sources only. Mushroom search
results are heavily polluted by AI content-farm sites (fungiatlas.com, mushroomtracker.ca,
sporecast.com, plantiary.com, zombiemyco.com, mushroomidentifiers.com, and similar) — never cite
them. Note the flip side: those farms *ranking* is evidence this niche is winnable by a site with
real expertise and real data.

## Part 1 — Dossiers for the current catalogue

### Morel (Morchella esculenta)

*Taxonomy note:* the North American yellow morel is now usually called *M. americana* (syn.
*M. esculentoides*); *M. esculenta* strictly is European. Pick one display name, footnote the other.

- **ID:** honeycomb of pits and ridges (pitted, not wrinkled); sliced lengthwise it is **one
  clean hollow chamber** base to tip; cap fused to the stem at its bottom edge; grows from soil.
- **Lookalikes:** *Gyromitra esculenta* (false morel) — brain-like wrinkles, chambered cottony
  interior (toxic). *Verpa bohemica* — cap attached only at the very top, hangs like a thimble
  on a pencil.
- **Season/habitat:** spring (Mar–Jun by latitude/elevation). Dying elms, ash, old orchards,
  cottonwood river bottoms; in the West, mass "burn morel" flushes the first spring after
  wildfire — directly relevant to Utah/Rockies users.
- **Uses:** choice edible but **toxic raw or undercooked** — Montana 2023 restaurant outbreak:
  51 ill, 2 deaths, associated with raw morels ([CDC MMWR](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10948192/)).
  Dries and reconstitutes superbly; advise moderation with alcohol on first tries. Long thought
  uncultivatable; large-scale outdoor cultivation succeeded in China in the 2010s.
- **Memorable fact:** burn-morel booms are big business — commercial crews chase the previous
  summer's wildfires across the West every spring.

### King Bolete (Boletus edulis)

- **ID:** pores not gills — **white young, aging yellow-olive, never red**; pores don't bruise
  blue; fine **white netting (reticulation)** on the upper stem; thick club stem, brown
  "hamburger-bun" cap; white flesh, no color change; olive-brown spore print.
- **Lookalikes:** *Tylopilus felleus* (bitter bolete) — pores flush pink, coarse dark netting,
  overwhelmingly bitter taste-and-spit (harmless, ruins the meal). Beginner rule: skip any
  bolete with red/orange pores or strong blue bruising.
- **Season/habitat:** summer–fall with conifers; in the Rockies it flushes late Jul–Sep with
  monsoon rain at high elevation.
- **Uses:** one of the world's premier culinary mushrooms; drying intensifies flavor. Cannot be
  cultivated (mycorrhizal) — all porcini is wild-harvested. Cook before eating.
- **Memorable fact:** "porcini" means "piglets," and North America's *B. edulis* is a species
  complex — the Rockies' red-capped version was only named *Boletus rubriceps* in 2014.

### Rainbow Chanterelle (Cantharellus roseocanus)

- **ID:** **false gills** — blunt forking ridges running down the stem, not removable blades;
  young cap margin with a **pinkish bloom** over bright orange; solid whitish string-cheese
  flesh; apricot-like odor; grows from soil, never shelving on wood.
- **Lookalikes:** *Hygrophoropsis aurantiaca* (false chanterelle) — thin crowded **true** gills,
  orange throughout. Jack-o'-lantern (*Omphalotus*) — true gills, dense clusters on wood, toxic.
  *Turbinellus floccosus* — scaly orange vase, GI upset.
- **Season/habitat:** late summer–fall; coastal Sitka spruce in the PNW but also Engelmann
  spruce in the Rockies — this is the chanterelle Utah-area foragers actually meet.
- **Uses:** choice edible; dry-sauté handles the moisture; not cultivatable. Chanterelles are
  among the few natural non-fortified food sources of vitamin D2.
- **Memorable fact:** the "rainbow" bloom fades with rain and age — the same patch can look
  like a plain golden chanterelle a week later.

### Lobster Mushroom (Hypomyces lactifluorum)

- **ID:** not a species but a **parasitic mold encasing a host mushroom** (*Russula brevipes* /
  *Lactifluus piperatus*); hard red-orange rind with finely pimpled texture; gills reduced to
  blunt ridges or absent; dense firm white interior; often half-buried in duff.
- **Lookalikes:** essentially none once the crust is known — but the host is unidentifiable.
  State the reasoning explicitly: evidence to date is the parasite targets those two
  (non-dangerous) hosts, and poisoning is essentially unrecorded. Reject old specimens (soft,
  purple-tinged, fishy).
- **Season/habitat:** mid-summer–fall in conifer forests wherever *R. brevipes* grows; common
  in the West including the Rockies.
- **Uses:** excellent firm edible, mild seafood note, holds texture in stews; the pigment is
  used for fabric dyeing. Not cultivatable. Cook it.
- **Memorable fact:** the rare parasite that upgrades its victim — *R. brevipes* is famously
  bland, but infected it becomes a commercially sold delicacy.

### Oyster Mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus)

- **ID:** fan-shaped caps shelving **on wood**; white-cream gills **running down** a stubby
  off-center stem; **white to pale lilac-gray spore print**; pleasant sweet/anise odor.
- **Lookalikes:** jack-o'-lantern — orange (oysters never are), toxic. *Pleurocybella
  porrigens* (angel wings) — thin stemless white fans on **conifer** wood; implicated in fatal
  encephalopathy in Japan in patients with kidney disease — advise avoiding. *Crepidotus* —
  small, flabby, **brown** spore print. Elm oyster — gills stop at the stem (edible anyway).
  Core checks: pale spore print + decurrent gills + on wood.
- **Season/habitat:** cool seasons, spring/fall and winter thaws, on hardwood logs and snags;
  in the Interior West look on cottonwood, aspen, box elder in river bottoms.
- **Uses:** very good edible (cook). **The classic beginner cultivation species** — straw,
  coffee grounds, kits. Contains small natural lovastatin — frame cholesterol claims as "of
  research interest," not medicine. A workhorse of mycoremediation research.
- **Memorable fact:** it's a carnivore — oyster mycelium paralyzes and digests nematode worms
  for nitrogen.

### Shaggy Mane (Coprinus comatus)

- **ID:** tall cylindrical white cap with shaggy upturned scales ("lawyer's wig"); gills white
  → pink → black as the cap **dissolves into ink from the rim upward** — a built-in
  confirmation; loose movable ring; black spore print; disturbed ground.
- **Lookalikes:** *Coprinopsis atramentaria* (common inky cap) — smooth gray-brown, no shag;
  contains **coprine**, causing a disulfiram-like alcohol reaction up to ~72 h after eating
  (shaggy mane doesn't). *Chlorophyllum molybdites* — parasol shape, gills never dissolve,
  **green spore print**, poisonous. Always cut lawn "buttons" vertically (Amanita check).
- **Season/habitat:** late summer–fall, lawns and disturbed soil worldwide; very common along
  the Wasatch Front after fall rains.
- **Uses:** good delicate edible **only young and all-white**; must be cooked within hours —
  it autodigests even refrigerated. Its ink genuinely served as writing ink. Blood-sugar
  research is animal-model only — strictly preliminary.
- **Memorable fact:** shaggy manes are strong enough to buckle asphalt.

### Gem-studded Puffball (Lycoperdon perlatum)

- **ID:** white golf-ball-to-pear body with tiny **detachable cone spines** that rub off
  leaving a net pattern; narrowed sterile base; sliced vertically the interior is **pure
  homogeneous white** with no internal structure; on soil.
- **Lookalikes:** **Amanita buttons** — the deadly one: any cap/gill/stem silhouette in the
  slice means put it down; the slice test is mandatory for every puffball eaten. *Scleroderma*
  earthballs — tough rind, purple-black firm interior (toxic). Yellow/olive interior — past
  edible, GI upset.
- **Season/habitat:** summer–fall, woods, trail edges, disturbed ground; common in mountain
  conifer forests.
- **Uses:** edible young and white throughout; slice, check, sauté (mild, takes other
  flavors). Sidebar-worthy caution: inhaling mature spore clouds can cause lycoperdonosis, a
  rare lung inflammation — "don't huff puffballs."
- **Memorable fact:** *Lycoperdon* translates as "wolf fart," and mature puffballs are
  raindrop-powered — each drop strike pumps out spores.

### Hedgehog Mushroom (Hydnum repandum)

- **ID:** **soft spines/teeth** under the cap instead of gills or pores, detaching easily when
  rubbed; dull orange-tan lumpy cap; brittle whitish flesh; white-cream spore print; no ring,
  no volva; from soil.
- **Lookalikes:** one of the safest groups — no dangerous toothed soil lookalikes. *H.
  umbilicatum* — smaller, dimpled center (equally edible). *Sarcodon imbricatus* — big dark
  scaly cap, often bitter. Woody *Hydnellum* — inedible by texture.
- **Season/habitat:** late summer–fall (winter on the West Coast); conifer and mixed forests,
  often in moss.
- **Uses:** choice edible, chanterelle-adjacent; cook; older caps can turn bitter. Not
  cultivatable.
- **Memorable fact:** despite the teeth, DNA places hedgehogs in the same order as
  chanterelles — which is why they taste like cousins.

### Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus)

- **ID:** a single unbranched white cushion of long (1–5 cm) **dangling icicle-like spines**;
  no cap, gills, or stem; grows from wounds in living hardwoods or on logs; pure white when
  prime, yellowing with age; white spore print.
- **Lookalikes:** only its edible siblings — *H. americanum* (branched, long spines) and
  *H. coralloides* (branched, comb-like spines). Nothing dangerous resembles it: a genuinely
  beginner-safe genus.
- **Season/habitat:** late summer–fall on hardwoods; in the Interior West mostly cottonwood
  and aspen.
- **Uses:** choice edible, crab/lobster texture (sear in slabs); cook. **Beginner-cultivatable**
  (kits, sawdust blocks). Medicinal status honestly: hericenones/erinacines stimulate nerve
  growth factor in lab/animal models; human trials are **small, short, and mixed** — promising
  but preliminary, not a treatment
  ([ADDF review](https://www.alzdiscovery.org/cognitive-vitality/ratings/lions-mane),
  [2023 pilot RCT](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10675414/)).
- **Memorable fact:** in the UK it's legally protected — picking wild lion's mane is
  prohibited there, while Americans grow it on kitchen counters.

### Fly Agaric (Amanita muscaria) — toxic

- **ID:** red (in North America often orange-red; var. *guessowii* yellow) cap with **white
  warts** — loose veil patches rain can wash off; free white gills, **white spore print**;
  skirt-like ring; bulbous base with **concentric scaly rings** rather than a clean sack.
- **Lookalikes:** *A. pantherina* (panther cap) — brown, same syndrome, typically stronger.
  Rain-washed caps can pass for edible russulas or European *A. caesarea* — the
  warts + ring + basal-rings combo is the tell.
- **Season/habitat:** summer–fall; worldwide with conifers and birches; abundant in Utah's
  spruce-fir zone.
- **Why people care:** **ibotenic acid** (glutamate agonist) and **muscimol** (GABA-A agonist)
  → delirium, drowsiness, occasionally seizures/coma; deaths rare, ER visits real. The FDA
  issued a 2024 memo amid the boom in unregulated "Amanita/muscimol" edibles
  ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/media/184512/download)). Cultural history: Siberian ritual use;
  named for its old use as a fly poison (caps in milk). The Santa-and-reindeer origin story is
  popular but speculative folklore — label it as such.
- **Parboiling flag:** see Part 4 — present both positions; do not present it as an edible.
- **Memorable fact:** the most-depicted mushroom on Earth — the 🍄 emoji, the Mario power-up,
  and most fairy-tale toadstools are all this species.

### Destroying Angel (Amanita bisporigera) — deadly

- **ID (to recognize and avoid):** pure white throughout; free white gills + **white spore
  print**; skirt-like ring; **sack-like volva at the base, often buried — always dig up the
  base**. Beginner takeaway: white gills + ring + volva = walk away.
- **Range flag:** *A. bisporigera* is the **eastern** North American destroying angel; the
  group includes *A. ocreata* (western NA, spring, oaks) and *A. virosa* (Europe). Treat
  "destroying angel" as a species group on a worldwide site.
- **Mistaken-for direction:** people mistake it for puffballs (slice test), meadow mushrooms
  (*Agaricus*: gills pink→chocolate, brown spore print, no volva), and white parasols — each
  pairing needs its specific check on the page.
- **Why it's medically urgent:** amatoxins (α-amanitin) shut down RNA polymerase II; liver
  cells die first. The trap is the **delay**: no symptoms 6–24 h, violent GI phase, a deceptive
  "recovery," then liver/kidney failure days 3–5. Amatoxins are heat-stable — **cooking does
  nothing**. Outcomes depend on early treatment — "call Poison Control before symptoms" is the
  page's core message ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK431052/)).
- **Memorable fact:** α-amanitin's precision as an RNA-polymerase inhibitor is being turned
  into targeted cancer therapy (amanitin-antibody conjugates in drug development).

### False Morel (Gyromitra esculenta) — toxic

- **ID:** reddish-brown **brain-like wrinkled** cap — folds and lobes, never honeycomb pits;
  cut lengthwise the interior is **chambered with cottony wisps**, never one clean hollow;
  spring, on soil under conifers, often near snowmelt — overlapping morel season and habitat,
  which is exactly the problem.
- **Lookalikes:** true morels (pitted + hollow — teach the side-by-side cut photo);
  *Gyromitra montana/gigas* ("snow morels," regarded as less toxic but debated); *Verpa*.
- **Why people care:** contains **gyromitrin**, metabolized to **monomethylhydrazine** —
  literally a rocket-propellant compound. GI onset ~6–12 h, then neurological symptoms up to
  seizures, hemolysis, liver injury; historically a major cause of European mushroom deaths.
  Even cooking fumes have poisoned cooks — ventilation warnings are traditional. MMH is an
  animal carcinogen ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470580/)).
- **Scandinavian tradition flag:** see Part 4 — present the tradition and the modern caution
  together; the site should not endorse eating it.
- **Memorable fact:** its epithet *esculenta* means "edible/delicious" — the most ironic name
  in mycology, on a mushroom whose toxin fueled rockets.

## Part 3 — The safety canon (what the section must teach)

### Universal beginner rules

1. **100% certainty from multiple features** — cap, underside type, stem, base (dig it up —
   volvas hide underground), spore print, substrate, habitat. One matching photo is not an ID.
2. **Learn the killers first:** death cap, destroying angels, deadly Galerina — on sight,
   before eating anything.
3. **Beginner prohibition:** never eat a gilled mushroom with white gills + white spore print +
   a ring and/or sack-like volva (the Amanita pattern); skip all little brown mushrooms.
4. **Cook all wild mushrooms thoroughly.** Raw morels have killed. And cooking does **not**
   neutralize amatoxins — cooking is for edibles, not decontamination.
5. **Small amount first, one new species at a time,** no alcohol on a first trial, and keep an
   uncooked specimen in the fridge (paper bag, 48 h) in case of a reaction.
6. Reject old, waterlogged, buggy, or unidentifiable specimens; don't mix unknowns in one
   basket; avoid roadsides and sprayed lawns (mushrooms concentrate heavy metals).
7. **Folklore tests are worthless** — silver spoons, "animals ate it," peelable caps, smell.
   Apps and AI photo IDs are hints, never verdicts (best tested app: 49% accuracy); a local
   mycological club or NAMA identifier is the real check.

### The "Foolproof Four" (beginner hub framing)

Coined by Clyde Christensen (University of Minnesota, 1943): **morel, giant puffball, shaggy
mane, chicken of the woods.** Teach it *with* the modern caveats: morels have the Gyromitra trap
and must be cooked; every puffball gets sliced; shaggy mane's smooth cousin fights with alcohol;
chicken of the woods upsets some stomachs. Sensible modern extensions: hedgehogs, lion's mane,
oysters.

### Poison-response protocol (the /learn/safety page)

- **US Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222**, 24/7 — call *before* symptoms if a dangerous species
  is even possible. Pets: ASPCA Animal Poison Control (888) 426-4435.
- **Save the evidence:** whole mushrooms including the dug-up base, or meal leftovers — paper
  bag or wax paper (never plastic), refrigerated; photograph top, underside, stem, base,
  habitat.
- **The delayed-onset trap, in bold on every toxic-species page:** amatoxin and gyromitrin
  symptoms start **6–24 hours after eating**, often with a false recovery while the liver
  fails. Late GI symptoms after a wild-mushroom meal are an emergency; "I feel better now" is
  not reassurance.
- Expert ID during an incident: NAMA's volunteer toxicology identifiers
  ([Report a Poisoning](https://namyco.org/interests/toxicology/report-a-poisoning/)) and the
  "Poisons Help; Emergency Identification for Mushrooms & Plants" Facebook group, which
  physicians and poison centers actually use.

## Part 4 — Flagged disagreements (never resolve silently on the site)

1. **Gyromitra esculenta edibility.** Finland legally sells it fresh with mandatory warnings
   and preparation instructions (repeated boiling, water discarded, ventilation; barred for
   children and pregnant people) — yet parboiling does not reliably remove all gyromitrin, and
   recent toxicology pushes toward advising against any consumption. Present tradition and
   modern caution together.
2. **Amanita muscaria parboiling.** Rubel & Arora (2008) documented detoxification-by-boiling
   food traditions; mainstream guides, poison centers, and the FDA's 2024 memo still classify
   it as poisonous, and ordinary cooking does not detoxify it. Present both; never as an edible.
3. **Chicken of the woods GI reactions.** Substrate (conifer/eucalyptus) vs. individual
   sensitivity vs. undercooking — the literature is genuinely unsettled. Advise: hardwood-grown,
   well cooked, small first serving.
4. **Medicinal evidence framing.** Lion's mane: mechanistic/animal data solid, human RCTs small
   and mixed. Turkey tail: adjunct-only evidence (PSK in Japan). Chaga: no human trials plus
   oxalate-nephropathy case reports. Honest framing is both an ethical and an E-E-A-T
   requirement.
5. **Morel taxonomy.** *M. esculenta* (Europe) vs. *M. americana/esculentoides* (NA).
6. **C. molybdites phrasing.** The defensible claim is "most commonly *consumed* poisonous
   mushroom in North America" (poison-center data), not "most poisonings worldwide."
7. **Destroying angel identity.** A species group (*bisporigera* east / *ocreata* west /
   *virosa* Europe), not one mushroom — matters for a worldwide map.
