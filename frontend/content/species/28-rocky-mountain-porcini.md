## Identification

The cap is chestnut to deep red-brown and often looks polished when moist. Young pores are white and stuffed, then open and age yellow to olive. The thick club-shaped stem carries a fine pale net pattern, especially near the top. Cut flesh is white and should not rapidly turn blue.

Habitat narrows the identification: *B. rubriceps* is associated with high-elevation spruce and fir in the southern Rocky Mountains, including Utah, Colorado, New Mexico, and Arizona.

## Uses and ecology

Firm, sound specimens are highly regarded cooked mushrooms and can be dried for concentrated flavor. Check for insect tunnels and soft pore layers, which develop quickly. A pore surface does not automatically mean edible; stem ornament, bruising, taste, and regional bolete knowledge all matter.

The fungus exchanges nutrients with living conifer roots and commonly fruits after sustained summer moisture.

## Memorable fact

Rocky Mountain porcini was formally described as its own species in 2014 after long being folded into the European king bolete name.
