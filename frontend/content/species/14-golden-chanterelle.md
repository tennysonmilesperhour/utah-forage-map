## Identification

The cap is egg-yellow to deep gold, becoming wavy and funnel shaped with age. Underneath are thick, blunt ridges that fork and cross-connect as they descend the stem. They are folds of the mushroom's flesh, not separate blades. The interior is solid, pale, and often peels into fibrous strips.

A fruity odor can support an identification, but scent varies and is never sufficient. Substrate is critical: true chanterelles arise from soil as mycorrhizal partners, even when buried wood makes the setting confusing.

## Uses and ecology

Golden chanterelles are prized cooked mushrooms with a firm texture. The European name *C. cibarius* has historically been applied too broadly. North America, Asia, and other regions contain related golden species, so a photograph and common name should not be treated as a worldwide species determination.

Chanterelles exchange soil nutrients and water with living trees. That partnership is why they appear in recurring forest patches but resist ordinary farm-style cultivation.

## Memorable fact

The false gills are not attached structures; they are wrinkles molded from the cap's own flesh.
