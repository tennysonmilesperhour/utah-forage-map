## Why are lookalikes important?
A plant can resemble a familiar herb without sharing its safety or identity. Common names, leaf shape and smell are not sufficient on their own. [Poison Control](https://www.poison.org/articles/plant) documents serious mistaken-identity risks, including hemlock confused with wild carrots. This page helps you organize reference comparisons; it is not a key for identifying a plant in front of you.

## Start with the hazardous reference
In the [plant atlas](/herbs/atlas), review the toxic reference entries alongside the plant you intended to study. Use scientific names so similarly named species do not get conflated. Read the warning, regional sources and the whole set of field marks before comparing photographs.

| Reference question | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| Is the name precise? | Scientific name, synonyms and the source’s geographic scope |
| Is the whole plant documented? | Growth form, leaf arrangement, stem, flower or fruit when present |
| Are the plants at comparable stages? | Young foliage and mature flowering material may look very different |
| Which part is discussed? | Leaves, roots, seeds, flowers and fruits must not be treated as interchangeable |
| What is still missing? | Any feature that requires another visit or expert examination |

## What can comparison photographs actually do?
They can show examples of structures and prompt better questions. They cannot represent every stage, individual variation, hybrid or regional relative. A comparison may help rule out a proposed name; it cannot make an incomplete record complete. Keep uncertain plants out of food collection and avoid tasting or rubbing an unknown plant as an identification experiment.

## Why region and season matter
Use [regional herb references](/herbs/regions) and locally appropriate floras. A profile from another continent may omit the lookalike that matters where you live. Likewise, missing flowers or fruit can prevent a confident determination even when a plant looks familiar. Revisit for observation when it is safe and permitted rather than assuming the absent feature.

## Where does a suspected exposure belong?
It belongs with a poison service, not in a comparison tool. [Oregon Poison Center](https://www.ohsu.edu/oregon-poison-center/plants-mushrooms) warns against eating wild plants or berries without proper identification. In the United States, call 1-800-222-1222 for a potential exposure; elsewhere, use the local poison center or emergency service. The [herb field manual’s poison section](/herbs/fieldcraft#poison) collects response resources. Do not wait for a page, app or community reply to establish what happened.
